Before I ever wrote a line of production code, there was a beige tower in the
family room, a monitor roughly the weight of a small refrigerator, and a
blinking `C:\>` prompt that did absolutely nothing unless you knew the magic
words.

My earliest interactions with computers were MS-DOS games. My brother and I
would spend entire summers glued to that machine, taking turns and arguing
about whose turn it actually was. Our rotation was heavy on three classics:
Arctic Adventure, Pharaoh's Tomb, and Desert Tank Wars.

Arctic Adventure was our platformer of choice: four glorious CGA colors, and
somehow every single one of them was used for spikes. You'd inch toward a key,
misjudge a jump by one pixel, and hand the keyboard over in disgust. Repeat
until dinner.

  <img src="/images/msdos-games/pharaohs-tomb.png" class="img-responsive" alt="Pharaoh's Tomb screenshot with a red brick pyramid, rolling boulders, and spikes"/>

Pharaoh's Tomb was the same energy but with more boulders. Looking at these
screenshots now, I'm realizing my entire childhood gaming career was mostly
avoiding pointy things in 320x200 resolution.

<figure class="about-photo">
  <img src="/images/msdos-games/tank-wars.png" class="img-responsive" alt="Desert Tank Wars screenshot with two tanks on hilly terrain, angle, power, and wind settings"/>
  <figcaption>Desert Tank Wars. Angle 75, Power 200, and a headwind. What could go wrong?</figcaption>
</figure>

And then there was Desert Tank Wars, the crown jewel of sibling warfare. Pick
an angle, pick a power, account for the wind, and lob a shell across the map
at your brother's tank. I didn't know I was doing physics. I just knew that
Angle 75, Power 200, and an 11 mph headwind meant I was about to blow myself
up.

But honestly, the games were only half of it. The other half was the command
line itself. To play anything, you had to type things, and I could not stop
tinkering. I'd poke around directories, try commands I'd seen somewhere,
rename things just to see what happened. And every so often, "just to see what
happened" meant accidentally deleting a system file, and suddenly the family
computer wouldn't boot.

There's no motivator quite like knowing you broke the family computer and
your parents haven't found out yet. That's how I ended up learning DOS
commands: from a boot disk, in a mild panic, copying files back and editing
config files until Windows booted again. Good times, honestly.

Between racing to fix the computer before anyone noticed and grinding through
those games one impossible screen at a time, something clicked. That's where
my love of problem solving comes from. It's the same loop I still live in as
an engineer today: something's broken, nobody's coming to save you, read the
error, form a theory, try it, repeat. The stakes were just lower. Grounded,
instead of a production incident.

I still have my original 5.25" floppy disks, by the way. Nothing in my house
can read them anymore, but I'm keeping them anyway.
